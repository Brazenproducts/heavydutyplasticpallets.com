Plastic Pallet ROI vs Wood: A Cost-Per-Trip Breakdown

Heavydutyplasticpallets Editorial • October 2026

The question procurement teams ask most often about heavy duty plastic pallets is not whether they last longer than wood — everyone knows they do. It is whether the longer life justifies a purchase price three to five times higher. This analysis builds the cost-per-trip model that answers that question with numbers rather than opinion.

Why Unit Price Is the Wrong Metric

A wood pallet might cost $12 to $18, while a comparable heavy duty plastic pallet runs $45 to $90. Compared on sticker price alone, wood wins every time. But pallets are not consumed in a single use; they cycle. The meaningful metric is cost per trip, which divides the total lifetime cost of a pallet by the number of trips it completes before retirement.

Building the Cost-Per-Trip Model

Four inputs drive the model:

  • Acquisition cost — the purchase price per pallet.
  • Service life — total trips before the pallet is scrapped.
  • Repair cost — cumulative maintenance over that life.
  • Disposal and replacement — cost to retire and replace a failed unit.

A wood pallet averaging 15 to 20 trips at $15, with occasional $3 repairs, lands near $0.90 to $1.10 per trip. A heavy duty plastic pallet at $65 completing 150 to 250 trips with near-zero repair lands around $0.30 to $0.45 per trip. The heavy duty plastic pallets in closed-loop service routinely reach the high end of that trip range, pushing cost per trip below half the wood figure.

The Hidden Costs Wood Carries

The per-trip comparison above understates plastic's advantage because it ignores costs that never appear on a wood pallet invoice:

  • Fastener failures — protruding nails damage product, tear bags, and injure workers, generating claims and OSHA exposure.
  • Weight variance — wood pallets absorb moisture and gain weight, inflating freight cost on every loaded trip.
  • Contamination — wood harbors moisture, mold, and pests, disqualifying it from many food and pharma applications and triggering ISPM-15 treatment for export.
  • Inconsistent dimensions — warped wood jams automated conveyors and AS/RS equipment, causing downtime.

Where Plastic Pays Back Fastest

The ROI case is strongest in closed-loop systems where pallets return reliably, in automated facilities that demand dimensional consistency, and in food, beverage, and pharmaceutical operations where hygiene is non-negotiable. In pure one-way shipping with no pallet recovery, wood may still win, because plastic's durability is wasted when the pallet never comes back. Buyers evaluating a switch should start by measuring their actual pallet return rate. The broader Elipacko plastic packaging products line includes designs matched to closed-loop recovery, which is where the payback is quickest.

Running Your Own Numbers

To build a defensible business case, pull your real data: average wood pallet price, annual purchase volume, estimated trips per pallet, repair spend, product-damage claims tied to pallet failures, and any freight premium from moisture weight gain. Drop those into the cost-per-trip formula for both materials. Most food and consumer-goods distributors find the plastic pallet pays back within 18 to 36 months, after which every remaining trip is pure savings.

The switch is not automatic for every operation, but for any network that recovers its pallets, the cost-per-trip math usually favors plastic decisively once the full picture — not just the sticker price — enters the equation.
